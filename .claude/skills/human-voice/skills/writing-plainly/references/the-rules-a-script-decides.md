# The rules a script decides

Each rule in this list is one the check script reports on its own.

Every banned word in this list sits in backticks, because the check skips a backticked span. That is how a rulebook names the word it bans without reporting itself.

- `dash`. Write no em dash and no en dash. A comma, a colon or a new sentence does the same work.
- `empty-adverb`. Cut the adverb that adds length and no meaning. The list is `very`, `quite`, `really`, `basically`, `essentially` and `actually`.
- `there-is`. Open a sentence on its real subject rather than on `there is` or `there are`.
- `semicolon`. Split a pair of sentences joined by a semicolon into two. Two short sentences read faster than one long one.
- `long-sentence`. Keep every sentence to 25 words or fewer. A reader loses one that runs longer.
- `broken-paragraph`. Write each paragraph as one line, however long it runs. Markdown breaks the line at the window edge on its own.
- `plain-word`. Write the plain word rather than the hard one, because a reader stops to decode the hard word. The words sit in `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/skills/writing-plainly/scripts/lib/refused-words.mjs`, each with its plain word, its source and the day it was set. The list refuses some words only as a verb or only as a noun, and approves the same word in another use. The check reports one of those as a warning, so a reader judges which use the sentence has. It reports every other refused word as an error, and never reports a technical word this work keeps.
- `noun-for-verb`. Write the verb rather than a noun made from it, so `decide` rather than `make a decision` and `check` rather than `perform a check`. The phrases sit at the top of the rules library, `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/skills/writing-plainly/scripts/lib/text.mjs`, one line each with the verb to write in its place. Where the verb the noun came from is itself a refused word, the line names the plain word the data file gives for it. The message names the word on the line.
- `ing-tail`. End a sentence of plain facts with a full stop. The tail is a comma and a phrase built on a word ending in `ing`, such as `contributing to the development of the region`. It adds a judgement the sentence did not earn. A word such as `including` or `during` opens no tail, and the library has a list of those. The check reports this rule as a warning. An item of a list has the same form, and a reader judges which one the sentence has.
- `not-x-but-y`. Say what a thing is and stop, rather than saying first what it is not. The phrases are `not only X but Y`, `not just X, it's Y`, `it's not X, it's Y`, `rather than merely` and `no X, no Y, no Z`.
- `it-matters`. Show the result rather than telling the reader that something matters, because a reader decides what matters. The phrases are `matters because`, `distinction matters`, `what matters more`, `what matters is`, `it matters that` and `this matters`.
- `benefit-with-no-cost`. Name the cost rather than promising a benefit with none, because real work has a cost. The phrases are `without requiring`, `without sacrificing`, `without losing`, `without compromising`, `without needing` and `without having to`.
- `linking-word`. Start the next sentence rather than opening it on a formal linking word, because a writer often needs no linking word at all. The words are `furthermore`, `moreover`, `in addition`, `additionally`, `ultimately`, `consequently` and `subsequently`.
- `rating-word`. Write what the thing does rather than a word that rates it, such as how long it has run without failing. The words are `dependable`, `meaningful`, `profound`, `deliberate`, `robust`, `seamless`, `powerful`, `elegant`, `comprehensive`, `crucial`, `vital`, `pivotal` and `essential`.
- `serves-as`. Write `is` or `are` rather than a heavy verb that stands in for them. The phrases are `serves as`, `functions as`, `acts as`, `stands as` and `serves to`.
- `summary-repeat`. Delete a paragraph that says the section again, because the reader just read it. The check reports a sentence that opens on `in summary`, `in conclusion`, `to sum up`, `all in all`, `to summarize` or `overall,`. It reads the start of a line, of a list item and of a sentence after a full stop.
- `what-comes-next`. Do the thing rather than announcing it. The phrases are `looking ahead`, `in this section we will`, `in this section, we`, `what comes next`, `as we will see`, `as we'll see`, `in the following sections` and `let's dive in`.
- `weakening`. Take the position, or name the one thing that could not be found out. A phrase that weakens everything names nobody as the one who thinks it. The phrases are `may provide`, `not necessarily`, `some argue`, `some would say`, `it is worth noting`, `it's worth noting`, `it should be noted`, `it is important to note`, `arguably`, `to some extent`, `in some ways`, `could potentially` and `might possibly`.
- `title-case-heading`. Write a heading in sentence case, so only the first word and a name start with a capital letter. The check reads a line that opens with `#`. It reports one where three or more words after the first start with a capital letter. A word of one to three letters, such as `a`, `the` or `API`, counts for nothing, and so does a word inside backticks.
- `same-length`. Write a short sentence after a long one, on purpose, because sentence length that never changes reads as a template. For a text of at least eight sentences, the check measures how far the word count of each sentence sits from the average. It reports the text where that distance is small. It reports a warning once, on the first line of prose. A person judges rhythm, and the number only points at a text worth a look.
